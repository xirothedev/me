I joined The Resonance Team in April 2025. My first project was TaxEasy.

A year and a half later, I lead a team of five and still write code. I trust the runbook more than the architecture diagram now.

<Heading id="the-first-week" level={2}>
  {'The first week'}
</Heading>

The first week was a tax platform. The codebase was large, the domain was unfamiliar, and the users had deadlines. I spent the first few days reading the ingest pipeline and asking why each step existed.

Each step existed because a document can fail in a different way: an XML invoice, an SMS notification, a receipt image. The pipeline had a parser, an OCR step, a retry, and a human queue.

The first week taught me to read the pipeline as a history of failures.

<Heading id="the-first-platform" level={2}>
  {'The first platform'}
</Heading>

TaxEasy is a tax management SaaS. Five apps, a shared data layer, an email ingest pipeline, OCR, anomaly detection, and an observability stack. The constraint is the deadline. A bug is not a stack trace. It is a tax agent who cannot file.

The ingest pipeline is the part I know best. An email arrives, a worker picks it up, an XML invoice or a receipt image is parsed, the OCR result is checked against a schema, and the document is written to PostgreSQL. Each step can fail in a different way. The job state is the interface between the system and the person who has to fix it.

We cut the Docker build from about ten minutes to about thirty seconds with pnpm cache mounts and multi-stage builds. The platform passed 309 merged PRs in the main repository. The number is a record of volume. The useful measure is that the pipeline still runs on a Monday morning.

The other platforms came later: Ecopick, a marketplace and tournament system with state machines for rentals, listings, inspections, and refunds; XaDaoXa, a government e-services platform with a Zalo MiniApp and a staff CMS. Each had its own constraint. The hospital is the one that changed how I think about AI.

<Heading id="the-team" level={2}>
  {'The team'}
</Heading>

A team of five is small enough that everyone has to own something and large enough that the review queue is real.

The artifact I made legible was the pull request. A clear scope, the failure path, the test, and the runbook. The team does not need a speech. It needs to know what is owned, what is blocked, and what happens next.

I also learned to say no. A platform can absorb an infinite number of features. A team cannot. The useful question is not “can we build this?” It is “what will we stop doing if we build this?”

<Heading id="the-hospital" level={2}>
  {'The hospital'}
</Heading>

The hospital project changed how I think about AI.

In a hospital, the model is one part of a system that has to run on the hospital’s hardware, over the hospital’s documents, with the hospital’s people. The constraint is custody.

The stack follows that constraint: local inference, self-hosted retrieval, a thin proxy, a runbook. The model can be uncertain. The system has to show the evidence.

<Heading id="what-i-got-wrong" level={2}>
  {'What I got wrong'}
</Heading>

The runbook should ship with the feature. The operator needs it before the first restart, not after the pilot.

The work got smaller when the evidence got clearer.

<Heading id="what-i-would-do-again" level={2}>
  {'What I would do again'}
</Heading>

I would choose a team that owns the whole path: the API, the web app, the mobile app, the deploy, the runbook. The range is the point. It is hard to design a good failure path if you only see one layer.

I would keep writing. The writing forces me to explain the decision, and the explanation exposes the gaps.

I would keep the team small. Five people who own their work can do a lot. Five people who wait for instructions cannot.

<Heading id="what-is-next" level={2}>
  {'What is next'}
</Heading>

I am finishing my B.S. in Computer Science in 2029. I am leading the team, shipping code, and writing about the parts of production that do not fit in a launch post.

The next year is the hospital evaluation set, the next platform, and the next set of failures.
